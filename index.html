The methods behind the plan

Nothing here is exotic. The plan combines a few well-established ideas from motor-skill research and from generations of drum teaching, arranged so they fit into half an hour.

Deliberate practice
Work on one specific target just beyond your current ability, get feedback, adjust, repeat. This is the core finding of Anders Ericsson's research on expert performance. In this plan every block has one target ("paradiddles clean at 72 bpm"), and you check it by ear and by video.
The metronome, always
Time is a drummer's first job. Playing with a click builds an internal pulse; recording shows where you drift. You'll also practise without the click on purpose (gap click, below) so the pulse becomes yours.
The tempo ladder
Start at a speed where the pattern is easy, add 4 bpm after clean repetitions, hold or step back after mistakes. Slow, accurate practice builds the right motion; speed follows.
Rudiments and sticking vocabulary
The standard rudiments and the patterns in George L. Stone's Stick Control are the alphabet of hand technique. Fills, grooves and solos are built from them.
Relaxed, rebound-based stroke technique
Modern teaching, influenced by the Moeller tradition, treats the stick as something that bounces: you lift, let it fall, and let it rebound. Tension caps your speed and causes injuries. Each session starts with relaxed strokes for this reason.
Reading and syncopation
Ted Reed's Progressive Steps to Syncopation for the Modern Drummer has been a standard for decades: one page of rhythms you can read, orchestrate on the kit and use to build independence.
Groove first, chops second
Most playing situations reward a steady, musical groove far more than fast hands. Phase 1 to 3 build both, but grooves and songs appear from week 1.
Interleaving and spaced repetition
Rotating the focus by day (hands, feet, reading, grooves, fills) and returning to older material leads to better long-term retention than practising one thing in a single block. The weekly rotation is built for this.
Recording yourself
Video and audio show tension, uneven strokes and time problems that are invisible while playing. You'll record every Saturday and compare against your baseline at checkpoints.
Consistency and rest
Thirty minutes six days a week is about 150 to 170 hours over the year. Short daily sessions with sleep in between consolidate skill better than one long weekly session. The light day is for recovery, not a loophole.

How to use this document. Read the setup, baseline and session sections once. Then follow the current phase's weekly table day by day, use the exercise library as a reference, and tick days off in the tracker at the bottom. The tempos and targets are guidelines. If something takes longer, stay on it. Finishing a phase on time matters less than finishing it cleanly.

If noise is a constraint, do your hand work on the pad, and use an electronic kit, mesh heads or low-volume cymbals for the kit blocks. A quiet setup you use daily beats a loud one you avoid.

Kit and body position

  • Throne: high enough that your thighs slope slightly downward from hips to knees. Too low locks your hips and pushes you into your feet.
  • Snare: at a height where your forearms angle slightly downward to the head and your shoulders stay relaxed. Not so high that you lift your elbows.
  • Hi-hat and cymbals: reachable without stretching. Nothing should force your wrists to bend sharply.
  • Feet: the pedals close enough that your heels can rest or lift without your knees being pulled up or out.
  • Posture: sit tall, back neutral, shoulders down. Check yourself on video.

Grip

You probably learned matched grip. Hold the stick about a third of the way up from the butt, with the fulcrum between thumb and index finger. The back fingers wrap loosely and are used to add control and power. Think of holding a small bird: firm enough that it can't fly away, gentle enough that you don't hurt it. If the stick feels like it is fighting you, the grip is too tight.

This is general practice guidance, not medical advice. If you feel sharp pain, numbness or tingling in your hands, wrists, forearms or back, stop playing, and get it checked by a professional before continuing.

The 30-minute session

Every session has the same skeleton. What goes inside each block changes by day and phase, but you never have to decide how to spend your time.

Warm-up5 min
Technique8 min, pad
Kit8 min
Application6 min
Play3 min

The five blocks

1. Warm-up (5 min, pad)
Shake out and stretch your forearms for 30 seconds. Then one minute each of single strokes, double strokes and paradiddles at a comfortable tempo, and one minute of stroke-height control (below). The goal is relaxation, not speed. Finish with 30 seconds of shaking out.
2. Technique (8 min, pad)
The day's hand work: sticking patterns, rudiments or accent control. Use the tempo ladder. One or two patterns only, played with full attention.
3. Kit (8 min)
Grooves, kick patterns, hi-hat foot and independence. Add limbs one at a time: hands first, then kick, then hi-hat foot.
4. Application (6 min)
Use today's material in context: reading, fills, or a song section. This is where technique becomes music.
5. Play (3 min)
Play along with a song or make up grooves over a click. Saturday's play block is recorded. Spend the last 30 seconds writing your log line.

Ground rules

  • One target per block. If you try to fix five things, you fix none.
  • Start slower than your ego wants. Ladder up from a tempo where the pattern is easy.
  • Stop and reset if you tense up. Shake out your hands, drop the tempo, breathe.
  • Never miss twice. One skipped day is fine. Two in a row starts a habit change.
  • The minimum session is 10 minutes: the warm-up plus one exercise from today's technique block. On a bad day this still keeps the habit alive.
  • Always end with something fun. The last three minutes are yours.

Exercise library

Stroke-height control

The basis of dynamics. On the pad, play RLRL in 8ths, with accents at 9 inches and unaccented notes at 2 inches. Start slowly, then repeat with other accent patterns. The unaccented notes should always be quiet.

  • Full stroke: starts high, ends high. Loud, then back up.
  • Down stroke: starts high, stops low. Loud, then quiet.
  • Tap: starts low, stops low. Quiet, controlled.
  • Up stroke: starts low, ends high. Quiet, then loud.

Sticking patterns

Single stroke roll    R L R L R L R L
Double stroke roll    R R L L R R L L
Single paradiddle     R L R R   L R L L
Double paradiddle     R L R L R R   L R L R L L
Triple paradiddle     R L R L R L R R   L R L R L R L L
Paradiddle-diddle     R L R R L L
Five-stroke roll      R R L L R   (then L L R R L)
Seven-stroke roll     R R L L R R L   (then L L R R L L R)
Flam                  lR   rL      (small letter = soft grace note)
Flam tap              lR R   rL L
Flam accent           lR L R   rL R L
Drag (ruff)           llR   rrL

Practise every pattern starting with the right hand, then the left. Play them at a slow tempo, then use the ladder. Move them around the kit once they are relaxed.

Accent patterns

Play a steady stream of 16ths and accent one note in each group of four, then move the accent through all four positions. Then repeat with accents on every third note. The unaccented notes stay low.

Accent on 1:   R l r l   R l r l   (capital = accent)
Accent on 2:   r L r l   r L r l
Accent on 3:   r l R l   r l R l
Accent on 4:   r l r L   r l r L

The tempo ladder

  1. Choose a tempo where you can play the pattern perfectly, relaxed, four times in a row.
  2. Play four clean repetitions, then raise the metronome by 4 bpm.
  3. If you make a mistake, repeat the same tempo. After two misses in a row, drop back 4 bpm.
  4. When you can't stay relaxed, stop climbing. Play the last clean tempo twice more, and log it.
  5. Start the next session about 8 bpm lower than your log so you build up cleanly again.

Metronome games

  • Click on 2 and 4 only: the click becomes the backbeat; you supply the rest.
  • Click on 1 only: tests your ability to keep time through the bar.
  • Gap click: two bars with the click, two bars silent. Do you still land on the click when it returns?
  • Subdivision switching: play quarters, then 8ths, then triplets, then 16ths, one bar each, without changing the tempo.
  • Half-speed check: if a pattern isn't perfect, play it at half tempo for a minute before trying again.

Feet

  • Heel-down: heel rests on the pedal, ankle drives the beater. Controlled and accurate; best for early work.
  • Heel-up: heel lifted, the whole leg drives. More power and speed. Use it once heel-down feels relaxed.
  • Hi-hat foot: practise "chick" on 2 and 4 with the hands quiet, then add a groove. Listen to the foot; it is a real part of the groove.
  • Kick patterns: play a steady 8th-note hi-hat with a backbeat, and change only the kick. Use these:
1 + 2 + 3 + 4 +
X . . . X . . .    kick on 1 and 3
X . . . . . X .    kick on 1 and the + of 3? no: on 1 and the + of 2... see note
X . . . X . X .    kick on 1, 3, and the + of 3
X . X . . . X .    kick on 1, the + of 1... see note

Note: rather than counting these, set them up in the groove player above and listen. Tap a cell in the kick row to try your own patterns, then play them on the kit.

Coordination and independence

  • Add one limb at a time: hands only, then kick, then hi-hat foot. If a limb falls apart, remove it and rebuild.
  • Ostinato plus reading: keep a steady hi-hat or ride pattern with the right hand and read a Syncopation line with the left hand, plus a fixed kick pattern.
  • Sing or count aloud: speaking the pulse while playing keeps your internal count honest.
  • Orchestration: after reading a line on snare, play the same rhythm on kick, then toms, then split between limbs.

Reading

  • Say the rhythm aloud with counting before you play it.
  • Read one line at a time, at a tempo where you don't stop. Keep your eyes moving ahead of your hands.
  • Use Syncopation page by page: first on snare, then with kick on 1 and 3, then with hi-hat foot, then with a hi-hat or ride ostinato.
  • Don't repeat a page until you can read it without practising it. Switch pages regularly.

Groove library

Each groove has a mini grid. Press "Hear it" to load it into the groove player. The player then stays with you at the bottom of the screen. Slow it down, mute one limb, and play that limb yourself. Then play the whole groove.

Fill library

A fill is a short phrase that leads to the next section and lands on a crash and kick on beat 1. Learn each fill slowly on the pad or snare first, then move it around the toms. Use "+" for the "and". Sticking is R L unless stated.

Fill 1: two beats of 8ths (beats 3 and 4)
   3   +   4   +  | 1
   SD  SD  T1  T2 | CR+BD

Fill 2: 16ths on beat 4 only
   4  e  +  a   | 1
   SD SD T1 FT  | CR+BD
   R  L  R  L

Fill 3: 16ths around the kit, one beat per drum
   1        2        3        4
   SD x4    T1 x4    T2 x4    FT x4    (RLRL on each drum)

Fill 4: paradiddle fill, 16ths
   R L R R  L R L L  (start on SD, move to T1, T2, FT)

Fill 5: triplet fill (two beats)
   R L R  L R L   (1st triplet on SD, 2nd on T1)

Fill 6: flam accent fill
   lR L R  rL R L   (flams on the snare and toms)

Add each fill to your groove at the end of a four-bar phrase: three bars of groove, one bar of fill, crash on 1. Then try two-bar fills, and then fills that start on the "and" of a beat.
